The Question of Existence

An inquiry into the Matrix, consciousness, identity, meaning and freedom.

So far through my life experience as a conscious and aware being, I have arrived at a question that I cannot yet answer:

Why?

Why is existence the way it is?

Why does the harder situation sometimes seem easier to escape than the simpler one we expected to overcome without difficulty? Why, when I chase after what I understand as a successful life, does it sometimes feel as though the faster I pursue it, the faster I am dragged behind?

I have begun to wonder whether there is something about existence that I have not yet understood.

Perhaps the problem is not simply the circumstances themselves, but the relationship between myself and those circumstances. Perhaps what appears to be happening outside of me is connected to something happening within me.

This is where my understanding of the Matrix begins.

I do not yet claim to know exactly what the Matrix is. It is still an immediate belief, an intuition, a framework through which I am trying to understand my experience. I do not know whether it describes reality literally, symbolically, psychologically, spiritually, or through some combination of these.

But I increasingly feel that the Matrix represents the inner world as much as it represents the outer world.

The inner world may be projected into the outer world through our actions, choices, relationships, creations, expectations, fears, desires and interpretations. What exists within may therefore find some form of expression without.

And if this is true, then perhaps the physical world is not entirely separate from the psyche.

Perhaps the outer world is, at least in part, a reflection, projection, or creation of patterns that first exist within.

Am I living in the Matrix, or am I participating in its creation?

Perhaps I am both.

The Red Pill

I believe, so far, that I have taken the red pill.

But taking the red pill has not given me clarity.

It has given me confusion.

It has thrown me into what I can only describe as hell—not necessarily hell as a physical place, but hell as an experience of consciousness: confusion, darkness, uncertainty, confrontation and the collapse of things I once believed I understood.

This is where I encounter Amenti and Arulu.

I do not understand these as merely geographical places. In my philosophy they represent places of the psyche—states, depths, dimensions or territories within consciousness that I have not yet fully understood.

They represent the descent inward.

The darkness.

The unknown.

The places within myself that I may have previously avoided because I did not know how to confront them.

Perhaps the red pill does not immediately reveal the truth. Perhaps it first reveals how much of what I thought was truth was actually assumption.

And perhaps this is why awakening can feel more like falling than rising.

When an old structure collapses, the absence of that structure can initially feel like darkness.

The Greatest Battle

I have come to believe that the greatest battle in an individual's life will always be inward.

It will always, in some sense, be mental.

The external world may provide the battlefield, but the deepest conflict occurs within the person experiencing it.

It is the battle between:

  • the mind and the heart,
  • having and lacking,
  • potential and failure,
  • wisdom and ignorance,
  • acceptance and rejection.

The more I examine deep mysticism, the more I notice this recurring language of opposites.

Right and left.
Light and darkness.
Masculine and feminine.
Anima and animus.
The conscious and the unconscious.
Creation and destruction.
Attachment and release.

Perhaps these opposites are not separate enemies. Perhaps they are expressions of a deeper unity that I have not yet understood.

Maybe the battle exists because I perceive reality through opposites. Maybe consciousness itself is learning to hold two apparently contradictory things at once.

Or perhaps there really is a struggle between these forces.

I do not yet know.

But I know that the struggle is something I experience inwardly.

The Outer World

This leads me to question how much of my attention I should give to the physical world.

So much of human life seems to revolve around material things: possessions, status, achievement, money, recognition, appearance, relationships, success and failure.

But if the greatest battle is inward, then what if all of these things are secondary?

What if the physical world is not the source of the deepest conflict, but the stage upon which the inner conflict becomes visible?

Perhaps everything outside of me is not meaningless. Perhaps it is a projection. Perhaps it is a distraction. Perhaps it is both.

A person may create something physically, but before that creation existed in the physical world, it existed somewhere within the psyche—as an idea, desire, image, intention or possibility.

In this sense, the Matrix may move in both directions.

The inner world can become the outer world.

And the outer world can then enter the inner world and reshape it.

Perhaps there is therefore a continuous exchange between the two.

I create the world.
The world creates me.
And somewhere between the two is the mystery of who I actually am.

The Machine, the Projection and the Observer

This brings me to perhaps the most disturbing question I have encountered:

What am I?

Am I the machine?

Am I the contents being projected?

Am I the projection itself?

Or am I the observer of the machine and the projection?

When I experience a thought, I can sometimes become aware that I am thinking. When I experience an emotion, I can sometimes become aware that I am feeling. When I experience fear, I can sometimes observe the fear. When I experience desire, I can sometimes observe the desire.

So if I can observe these things, then what exactly is the "I" that observes them?

If my thoughts change, am I still myself?

If my beliefs change, am I still myself?

If my personality changes, am I still myself?

If my memories disappeared, would I still be me?

If I remove the masks I have accumulated throughout my life, what remains?

And how many masks are there?

How many programs are running within me that I mistake for myself?

Perhaps some of my desires are not truly mine. Perhaps some of my fears were programmed into me. Perhaps some of my beliefs were inherited. Perhaps some of my ambitions were given to me by society.

Perhaps some of the identity I call "myself" is simply a collection of experiences, memories, conditioning and adaptations.

But if I remove all of these layers, I arrive at another mystery.

Who am I beneath them?

What Am I Meant to Do Here?

This question follows naturally:

What am I meant to do here?

If existence is simply a sequence of experiences, then what makes one experience meaningful and another meaningless?

Is there meaning in everything that happens to me?

If I suffer, am I supposed to learn something from it?

If I fail, is the failure itself meaningful?

If I succeed, what am I supposed to understand from success?

If I lose something, was it simply loss—or was there something within the experience that I was meant to encounter?

I do not yet know.

I am hesitant to say that every experience necessarily has a predetermined meaning, because that too would be another assumption.

Perhaps meaning exists independently and I discover it.

Perhaps meaning is created by me.

Perhaps meaning emerges from the relationship between what happens and how consciousness responds to it.

Or perhaps there is no inherent meaning at all.

These possibilities remain open.

Do I Have Free Will?

And beneath all of this is the question of freedom.

Do I actually have free will?

If my thoughts are influenced by my experiences, my experiences by my environment, my environment by other people, and my desires by things I may not even consciously understand, then how much of my decision-making is truly mine?

If I choose something because I desire it, where did the desire come from?

If I reject something because I fear it, where did the fear come from?

If I believe something because I was taught it, can I truly call that belief my own?

Perhaps freedom is not simply the ability to choose.

Perhaps freedom begins with becoming conscious of what is influencing the choice.

Maybe the first step toward freedom is seeing the programs.

Seeing the masks.

Seeing the desires.

Seeing the fears.

Seeing the contradictions.

Seeing the Matrix.

And perhaps even seeing the observer itself.

But I am not yet certain.

I am still inside the question.

The Beginning

I therefore do not want to begin my philosophy by pretending that I have discovered the answers.

I want to begin with what I know directly:

I am conscious.
I experience.
I observe.
I think.
I feel.
I desire.
I suffer.
I create.
I question.

And somewhere within all of this, there is an "I" that I have not yet completely understood.

I do not know whether the world is a projection. I do not know whether the Matrix is literal, psychological, spiritual, symbolic, or all of these simultaneously.

I do not know whether Amenti and Arulu are destinations, states of consciousness, dimensions of the psyche, symbols of inner descent, or something beyond my present understanding.

I do not know whether every experience has meaning.

I do not know whether I possess free will.

I do not know whether I am the machine, the projection, the projected contents, or the observer.

But these questions have become impossible for me to ignore.

And perhaps philosophy begins precisely here:

not with certainty,

but with the courage to look into the darkness and ask,

"What is actually here?"
